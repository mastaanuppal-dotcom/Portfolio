01 · Featured engineering projects

Designed, built, and debugged.

Each entry is a case file: what it is, why it exists, how it was built, and what broke along the way. Expand any project for the full write-up.

What I built
A motorized burr grinder designed and fabricated from scratch: a 24 V DC motor driving precision burrs through custom mechanical couplings, mounted on a purpose-built wood base, with a prototype grind-by-weight system for repeatable output.
Why
Consistent grind size and dose are what make coffee repeatable, and consumer grinders hide all of the interesting engineering. Building one exposed every layer: motor selection, torque under load, alignment, vibration, and control.
How
  • Modelled the base, couplings and burr carrier in SolidWorks, then fabricated the wood base and mechanical couplings for stability and shaft alignment.
  • Selected and mounted a 24 V DC motor and matched it to the burr set's load.
  • Prototyped a grind-by-weight cut-off so the machine stops at a target mass instead of a timer.
  • Ran iterative motor-load and durability testing, adjusting alignment and coupling stiffness between runs.
Engineering problem
Grind quality depends on keeping the burr gap constant while the motor sees a varying load. Any coupling misalignment or base flex shows up directly as inconsistent particle size, so the mechanical design had to be stiff and concentric before any control work mattered.
What was difficult
Getting alignment right with hand-fabricated parts: small errors in the couplings caused vibration and uneven grinding under load, which only appeared during sustained testing rather than on the bench.
What I learned
Test under real load early. Mechanical tolerances gate everything downstream, and a control loop (grind-by-weight) can only be as consistent as the mechanism it controls.
Result
A working, stable grinder with consistent output validated through repeated load and durability runs, and a grind-by-weight prototype demonstrating dose control.

What I built
A fully populated and verified printed circuit board: components placed and soldered using both through-hole and surface-mount techniques, then brought up and validated against the schematic.
Why
Reading a schematic and turning it into a board that measures correctly is the core loop of electronics work. This was the first time I owned that loop end-to-end, from bare board to a signed-off functional test.
How
  • Interpreted the schematic and component footprints to plan placement and orientation (polarity, pin 1, reference designators).
  • Soldered through-hole parts, then surface-mount parts with precision technique and inspection.
  • Powered up in stages and verified the board with a DC power supply, multimeter and oscilloscope: continuity, rail voltages, then signals at test points.
  • Systematically isolated and reworked faults found during test.
Engineering problem
A board with dozens of joints has dozens of ways to fail silently. The problem was building a test procedure that finds cold joints, bridges and wrong-orientation parts quickly, without damaging the board by powering a fault.
What was difficult
Surface-mount soldering at small pitch, and then diagnosing symptoms that could have several root causes. Probing with the oscilloscope and reasoning back to the schematic was the only reliable way to separate assembly errors from real circuit behaviour.
What I learned
Inspection before power. Verify rails before signals. A disciplined bring-up order turns debugging from guesswork into a checklist, and good soldering technique is mostly heat management and patience.
Result
A fully functional board, verified joint-by-joint and signal-by-signal, with faults found in test traced to root cause and corrected.

What I built
A digital alarm clock implemented entirely in VHDL and demonstrated on a Terasic DE10-Standard board (Intel Cyclone V 5CSXFC6D6F31C6): cascaded time counters, a settable alarm, and a finite-state machine driving the board's inputs and displays. Built with a lab partner as the course's final project.
Why
A clock is the classic sequential-logic system: it needs clock division from the 50 MHz oscillator, modulo counters that carry into each other, an FSM for set/run/alarm modes, and comparison logic. It exercises every idea from the course in one piece of real hardware.
How
  • Designed the datapath as a chain of modulo counters (seconds → minutes → hours) with carry-out into the next stage, plus alarm registers and an equality comparator.
  • Modelled the control as a Moore FSM with explicit state encoding, driving the display and alarm output.
  • Wrote testbenches and simulated in ModelSim (VHDL-2008) and GHDL 4.1 before touching hardware.
  • Synthesized in Quartus Prime, pinned to the DE10-Standard, and debugged on-chip with SignalTap.
Engineering problem
Keeping a multi-stage counter, a settable alarm and user input consistent under one 50 MHz clock without glitches: every carry, reset and mode change has to be synchronous, and the design has to synthesize identically to how it simulated.
What was difficult
Tooling. A SignalTap capture appeared broken because the CLOCK_50 net had been typed by hand instead of selected through the Node Finder, which created a phantom unconnected clock pin. ModelSim needed VHDL-2008 enabled for expressions like not KEY(n) in port maps, and testbenches that worked with direct entity instantiation in GHDL had to be rewritten with component instantiation for Quartus.
What I learned
Simulation, synthesis and on-chip debug are three different tools with three different opinions about your code. Understanding why they disagree (language standards, instantiation styles, clock-net binding) is the real FPGA skill, on top of the logic design itself.
Result
Completed and successfully demonstrated on the DE10-Standard. The interactive figure runs the same counter cascade and alarm-compare logic in your browser.
